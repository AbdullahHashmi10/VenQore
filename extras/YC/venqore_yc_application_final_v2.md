### Company name
VenQore

### One-line description
A staged path to a full business operating system — starting with a mathematically-correct ERP/POS core, built cheap and lean to prove itself and fund the bigger build, aimed at eventually replacing the disconnected tool stack larger companies run on today.

---

### What are you building?

VenQore's end goal is a full business software suite — broad enough that a company, small or large, could run its entire operation on it instead of stitching together five or ten disconnected tools. We're building it in stages rather than all at once, because that would take more capital and time than we have, and because we want each piece validated by real usage before building the next. What exists today is Part 1: an ERP/POS system built around one obsessively-tested accounting core — every calculation verified to 20 decimal places across 1,000+ automated tests — plus AI features that remove manual data entry, like turning handwritten orders and voice notes directly into sales. It's priced low and built lean specifically to prove the model and fund what comes next.

---

### What problem are you solving?

My father runs a shop, and the ERP software he used was expensive, complicated, and didn't give him the reports he needed — that's what pushed me to build the first version. It's since been validated in two more small businesses. But the bigger problem we're targeting is at the scale of larger companies: they're paying for expensive, bloated ERP software, or running fragmented stacks of disconnected tools, when what they actually need is one system that's both cheaper and more correct than what they have today.

---

### Why this idea, why you?

I didn't start from a market report — I built the first version to fix my father's business, then kept extending it every time a real problem showed up: a recipe-costing module because we were selling raw materials and manufactured products from the same inventory, VenSynQ because I'd built a WooCommerce store myself and needed it to sync with the ERP. My background is linguistics, not computer science — I taught myself to build, and VenQore is my third solo-shipped product after Al Ujrat (WordPress-based marketplace) and Protocol VII (Node/React SaaS). Every feature traces back to a specific failure I hit myself or watched a real business hit — and the staged strategy (prove cheap, then build toward enterprise) is the same discipline applied to company-building, not just engineering.

### Traction

Live in 3 businesses today: my father's shop (2 months), a friend's business, and a relative's business — one paying, on a yearly growth-plan subscription. This is deliberately our proof stage, not our target market: small businesses validate that the core works and surface real bugs fast. The actual customers we're building toward are larger companies with real budgets, less price sensitivity, and a genuine need to consolidate scattered systems.

---

### Competition

For the current product, the real competition is Excel, WhatsApp-based order-taking, and cheap single-purpose apps. For where we're headed, the competition is expensive, complicated legacy ERP software that large companies already tolerate because switching feels riskier than staying — our bet is that being simultaneously cheaper and more mathematically reliable is a wedge into that market, not just the small-business one.

---

### Why now?

Multimodal AI models only recently became reliable enough to turn handwritten notes or voice messages into structured sales data, which AI Scan depends on. Marketplace APIs have matured enough (we already have Amazon SP-API approval) that multi-channel selling is now a mainstream problem, not a niche one. And the staged approach itself — prove cheap, then build toward enterprise-grade — is more viable now than it would have been a few years ago, because a solo founder can build and test a genuinely reliable core without needing a large team first.

---

### Long-term vision

The end state isn't an ERP add-on business — it's a full business operating system that larger companies eventually adopt to replace the fragmented, expensive stack they're currently running on. We're building toward that by proving the core is trustworthy and cheap to run today, then layering in what larger customers actually require — deeper integrations, more robust support, broader feature coverage — as we grow. Every stage funds the next one; we're not asking to skip straight to the end state, we're asking for the capital to move through the stages faster than bootstrapping alone would allow.
